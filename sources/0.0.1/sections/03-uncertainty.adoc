== Measurement Uncertainty in Hydrography

=== Introduction

* Uncertainty is used to support our understanding of the measurements (start with 3 examples). It's about managing uncertainties, because some aspects can be controlled.
* The importance of an uncertainty budget (prediction) and estimating the uncertainty from the data (data-driven).
* Contrary to land surveying techniques, the establishment of control points is mostly not feasible in hydrographic surveying. Hence, we cannot use the same metrics to evaluate accuracy of e.g. depth, current speed. Principles are the same but evaluation tools are not the same. Often, the error budgets will be smaller on land versus sea because we can better control the measurement factors.
* Accuracy should not be confused with uncertainty. Based on the (new) definition of S-32, Accuracy should be qualitative and be related to bias.

S-44 Edition 5 (IHO, 1998) advocated the general adoption of the term _uncertainty_ instead of _error_ in order to better align with the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM, 2008). This was reflected for example by the change from _Total Propagated Error_ (TPE) to _Total Progagated Uncertainty_ (TPU). The implications of this adaption are however greater than just a change in terminology. Rather, they warrant a change from a more traditional approach to error estimation (i.e. the Error Approach), in which a clear distinguish is made between random and systematic error estimation. In the recommended Uncertainty Approach, this distinction is not made as the nature of the error may not be subject to categorization. Indeed, an error may, for example, display a random nature in the short-term and a systematic nature in the long-term or vice-versa. Instead, the Uncertainty Approach attempts to assign an interval in which the unknowable true value of a measurement is expected to lie as defined within a particular confidence level, irrespective of the nature of that error (random or systematic).

The rest of this chapter is structured as follows. Section 2 will further describe concepts related to error theory as they should be applied to hydrographic surveying. Section 3 will describe how uncertainty should be applied in practice in hydrographic surveying. More emphasis is placed on depth measurement uncertainty, but it should be recognized that measurement uncertainty also applies to other hydrographic measurand such as surface current and tides.

=== Fundamentals of Error Theory

All measurements carry error; the “truth” is unknowable and only approached probabilistically. In hydrography this matters directly to safety of navigation and downstream products (ENCs, S-102 surfaces, contours, shoal-biased selections). Rather than treating a single accuracy number as definitive, modern survey practice expresses how far a measured value may be from the true value with a stated confidence level. This section sets error-theory foundations and then frames how we quantify and combine uncertainties for hydrographic use.

==== Concept of Error

*Error* is the difference between a measured value and the (unknown) true value. Two behaviors are distinguished:

* *Systematic error (bias)*: repeatable offset or trend (e.g., sound-speed model bias, tide/ellipsoid separation bias, GNSS antenna height mis-modeling). Predictable once detected; ideally corrected. Residual bias contributes to total error.
* *Random error (noise)*: zero-mean scatter from unmodeled variability (e.g., beam steering jitter, heave filtering residuals, electronic/photon noise, short-scale water-level variability). Averages down with repetition.

*Blunders* (gross mistakes, e.g., wrong lever-arm sign, wrong epoch) are not treated as “error”; they are data-quality failures to detect and remove via Quality Check.

==== Accuracy and Precision in Hydrography
:stem: latexmath

*Accuracy / bias*: proximity of the measured quantity to a reference or true value. Estimated via comparisons to control (reference surfaces, crosslines, junctions, check lines, control points) and by tracking known bias contributors (vertical datum realization, sound speed, latency).

* Precision / variance (standard deviation): repeatability under unchanged conditions. For depth, summarize precision as a standard deviation stem:[sqrt(4) = 2]

=== Uncertainty Applied to Hydrographic Surveying

Uncertainty is an inherent property of all measurements. For mariners, as navigation increasingly adopts explicit risk-management practices, quantified uncertainty becomes critical to decision-making—particularly where economic pressures on efficiency lead vessels to operate with reduced under-keel clearance and closer to charted depths. Consequently, surveyors are increasingly required to understand, manage, estimate, and report uncertainty.
In hydrography, uncertainty is commonly classified by (i) when it is evaluated and (ii) how it is evaluated.

1) Timing of evaluation

*A priori uncertainty*. Estimated before data acquisition, typically from manufacturer specifications, laboratory calibration results, and modelling of known error sources. Because these estimates do not use survey data, they may not reflect the specific environmental and operational conditions of the survey area.
	
*A posteriori uncertainty*. Estimated from the acquired survey data, often by aggregating one or more parameters derived from the observations (e.g., line-to-line or surface differences). These estimates generally better represent the uncertainty actually achieved during the survey.

2) Method of estimation

*Model-based approach*. Makes use of mathematical models to derive uncertainty from known sources of variance. This approach can be applied a priori for planning and acceptance criteria and then refined a posteriori with data collected, survey-specific calibrations, etc.
	
*Empirical approach*. Estimates uncertainty directly from the dispersion of observations, such as repeat lines, crosslines, beam-to-beam or line-to-line differences, surface-difference statistics, or algorithmic estimators (e.g., gridding methods that output per-node uncertainty). This approach is inherently a posteriori because it requires the collected data.

Practical considerations

Model-based estimates often adopt conservative inputs to encompass all identified error sources and therefore may overestimate the true uncertainty. Empirical estimates can underestimate uncertainty if systematic effects or unobserved biases are not captured by the available data (e.g., uncompensated offsets, spatially correlated refraction errors, unmodelled timing biases). Best practice is to use both: employ a priori TPU for planning and initial compliance with standards, then confirm and—where appropriate—refine uncertainty a posteriori. Use empirical methods to validate final estimations (e.g., crossline analysis and surface consistency assessments). Final reporting should state the basis of the uncertainty estimate (model-based vs. data-driven, or hybrid), the coverage to which it applies, and any assumptions or limitations.

==== Estimating Depth Uncertainties

Estimating depths are inherently uncertain due to a combination of instrument limitations, environmental variability, and processing methods. Understanding and quantifying these uncertainties are essential to ensuring safety of navigation, critical for producing reliable bathymetric products and instrumenting scientific- and engineering decision making. 

The predictive and data-driven approaches are fundamentally different in the sense that the predictive approach will estimate an uncertainty for each sounding measurement while the data-driven approach will estimate the uncertainty from a statistical computation from multiple sounding measurements within an appropriate area or node. Each method has distinct purposes, advantages, and limitations, and they are most effective when used together. 

The predictive approach estimates the uncertain of depth measurements based on the knowledge of system uncertainties, sensor capabilities, and environmental factors. This approach provides per-sounding uncertainty, which is beneficial during acquisition and processing. Since the approach relies on accurate characterization of each source of uncertainty, it tends to conservatively overestimate the combined uncertainty. 

The data-driven approach estimates uncertainty after depth measurements have been acquired, using statistical analysis of the measured depths. Instead of collecting each source of uncertainty, the approach relies on actual measurement dispersion to infer the reliability of depth estimates within areas or nodes. This approach directly reflects the variability of depth measurements and useful when producing bathymetric products. However, it tends to underestimate uncertainty, as it neglects systematic uncertainties which effects all depth measurements. In addition, the data-driven approach requires sufficient data density to produce valid statistical estimates of uncertainty.

===== Predictive Approach

In hydrographic surveying, understanding and minimizing TPU, TVU, and THU are crucial. These components influence the reliability of the data and, consequently, the safety and efficiency of maritime operations.  

Total Propagated Uncertainty (TPU) is a comprehensive measure used in hydrography to quantify the overall predicted uncertainty/error in three dimensions associated with the positioning and measurement of hydrographic data e.g. a sounding on the seafloor. It encompasses various sources of error that can influence the accuracy of hydrographic surveys, including sensor inaccuracies, expected environmental conditions, model inaccuracies and data processing methods. Instead of TPU, the term “error budget” is also used. TPU is particularly relevant in the context of survey preparation and execution were it can be used as a tool to asses if the specified requirements of a survey is expected to be met using a specific set-up that is intended to measure hydrographic data.

#All measurements are considered to be uncorrelated random variables with normally distributed uncertainties, all uncertainty sources acting independently, so all covariance terms can be disregarded, and the TPU can be computed statistically using the well-known Law of Uncertainty Propagation.# 

The two primary components of TPU are Total Vertical Uncertainty (TVU) and Total Horizontal Uncertainty (THU).  

TVU refers to the cumulative vertical uncertainty/error in measurements, whereas THU refers to the horizontal cumulative uncertainty/error in measurements.  Uncertainties can be caused by factors such as uncertainties in vessel and instrument positioning, sound velocity variations, equipment calibration errors, vessel motion, tide corrections or uncertainty in the bending of light in the water when using techniques like ALB.
